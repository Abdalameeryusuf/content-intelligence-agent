## Claim

> "Everything I've started around products I've failed in the same area, which is I
> don't have distribution. I don't have an audience to sell those products to. So I
> can create the best product in the world, I can pay the best developers, the best
> designers, the best industrial engineers to build the coolest products, but if I
> don't have distribution to sell those products into, I'm cooked." — 2:13

## Their reasoning

He frames paid acquisition as the only alternative to owned distribution, and argues
the unit economics usually fail: a $50 hoodie costing $20–25 to make, plus $10–20 to
acquire the customer, plus shipping, returns, mistakes and product R&D, puts you
"upside down... losing money on every single customer."

He adds a second objection beyond economics: acquired customers are not sticky. Someone
who saw one ad and converted on one product "has no brand loyalty."

The conclusion he draws is sequencing, not just channel choice — build the loyal
audience *first*, around a common topic, then use it to feed the brands you own "for
free."

## Evidence they offered

**Personal, and negative.** He cites his own repeated failures — content businesses,
freelancing, product businesses — as the evidence: "all those businesses have kind of
failed." He offers no external data, no case studies, and no numbers from the failures
themselves. The hoodie unit economics are illustrative arithmetic, not reported figures.

At the time of recording he states he is "kind of at day zero," with ~50K TikTok
followers after three months, and describes himself as having no capital and no
meaningful traction.

## Scope / conditions

He scopes it to businesses selling products to consumers, and explicitly to his own
situation of having no capital. He does not claim it holds for businesses with
funded acquisition budgets or existing enterprise channels.

## Agent notes

This is the single most directly relevant insight in the pilot for Lifepal, and worth
flagging as such.

[INFERENCE] The situation is closely analogous: a solo builder, no capital for paid
acquisition, a product not yet launched, and a stated goal of 200–300 invested waitlist
signups. Kallaway's argument implies the waitlist *is* the distribution layer, and that
building it before launch is not a marketing nicety but the thing that determines
whether the launch has anywhere to land.

[INFERENCE] The disanalogy is worth stating: he is describing physical consumer
products with hard unit economics. Lifepal is software, where marginal cost is near
zero and the "upside down" arithmetic does not apply the same way. The distribution
argument survives that difference; the unit-economics argument does not transfer
directly and should not be cited as if it did.

[INFERENCE] Note the evidential weakness. This is a confident claim from someone who,
at the moment of stating it, had not yet succeeded at it — he is describing a plan, not
a result. `confidence: high` records that he said it clearly. It should not be read as
validation. Whether he executed this plan is checkable against the later catalog and is
a specific gap worth filling.
