## The pattern

Tell a success story by locating the moment it nearly did not happen, then attribute
the reversal to an outside party making a small, specific, contrarian decision.

The structure is: **success state → "but the real question is how" → near-death fact →
the small decision that reversed it.**

The near-death fact is the payload. Without it, the story is a growth chart.

## As executed here

- 0:20 — the pivot to mechanism: "but the real question is, how'd they do it? How did
  Stanley cups go from a construction worker's thermos to the dream Christmas gift?"
- 0:28 — the near-death fact: "in 2019 Stanley was actually about to discontinue the
  cup"
- 0:34 — the reversal, credited to outsiders: a group of mom bloggers, The Buy Guide,
  cut an affiliate deal to bulk-order 5,000 cups **on the condition Stanley make them
  in pastel colours**
- 0:43 — the outcome: sold out in five days, and "completely changed their philosophy
  on design"

The specificity is doing the work: not "influencers helped," but a named group, a
number, and a named condition.

## Why it works

A success that was always going to happen carries no tension. Establishing that it
nearly ended converts the outcome into something contingent, and contingency is what
makes a business story a story.

Crediting outsiders rather than the company adds a second effect: it implies the
company did not understand its own product, which is both more surprising and more
flattering to the audience's judgment.

## Where it fails

- **When there is no genuine near-death moment.** Inventing or inflating one is
  straightforwardly dishonest and is the main abuse of this structure.
- **When the reversal was systemic rather than a single decision.** Most real reversals
  are; compressing them into one decision is a distortion the format invites.
- **Survivorship.** The same pastel-colour bet made by a company that then failed is
  never told. The structure silently selects for winners.

## Transfer to Lifepal

**Directly, with the honesty constraint intact.** v0 has a genuine near-death moment
that is documented rather than reconstructed: the decision point where extending v0 was
the obvious path, and the export data instead said stop.

The difference — and it is the interesting one — is that Ameer's story inverts the
template. Stanley was saved by outsiders spotting value the company missed. Ameer's
reversal came from his *own* data contradicting his own intention. That is rarer and
harder, and it does not need the outsider.

[RECOMMENDATION] Do not force the outsider role into the Lifepal version. The
self-inflicted reversal is the more distinctive story and the honest one.
