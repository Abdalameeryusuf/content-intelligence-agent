## Claim

> "When people think deepfakes, they immediately imagine business leaders, politicians
> and celebrities being extorted and blackmailed, and we'll definitely have some of
> that. But that's not the most dangerous thing in my opinion." — 0:27

> "The most dangerous thing is all the engineered virality and the fake product reviews
> that are going to surface. We're entering an era where companies will start using
> indistinguishable AI personas to sway the public opinion around their products, and
> it'll become really hard to know who and what to trust." — 0:36

## Their reasoning

He argues the discourse is focused on the spectacular case (extortion of prominent
people) while the higher-volume harm is mundane: synthetic personas producing reviews
and manufactured consensus at scale.

The implicit mechanism is volume and plausibility — a fake persona endorsing a product
is unremarkable and therefore unchecked, where a fake video of a head of state invites
scrutiny.

## Evidence they offered

**A demonstration, not data.** He shows AI-generated people from Flux, then the same
images animated with Runway ML, and notes the only tell was text on a lanyard. That
establishes the capability exists; it does not evidence the predicted use.

No data on the prevalence of synthetic reviews, and no source.

None stated. Framed as a near-term trajectory.
